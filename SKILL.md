# Anti-Defensive Writing

Make the paper's strongest supported contribution easy to understand. Replace
apology, redundant caveats, and imagined objections with precise claims, relevant
evidence, and a coherent argument. Confidence comes from specificity and support.

## Match the Revision Scope

Apply this skill to English manuscript and reviewer-response text; the request
itself may be in any language. If the text to revise is not English, do not apply
this skill; handle the request normally.

Exclude all translation requests from this skill. Converting an already-written
non-English passage into English is translation, even when polishing is also
requested. Drafting new English text from notes or bullet points in any language
remains in scope, such as composing an English rebuttal from Chinese key points.
Distinguish these tasks by whether the user wants an existing passage translated
or new prose composed from source ideas.

Return edited or drafted manuscript and reviewer-response text in English.
Write explanatory notes in the user's language.

Infer the level of intervention from the request and supplied material:

- **Language polishing:** Improve wording and local flow while preserving the
  contribution, technical meaning, and substantive claims.
- **Shortening:** Remove repetition and low-value exposition while retaining the
  conditions and evidence needed to interpret the argument.
- **Structural revision:** Reorganize paragraphs or sections around the strongest
  supported contribution. Reframe the central story when the request permits it.
- **Experiment presentation or planning:** Connect each experiment to a claim or
  unresolved question. Distinguish completed findings from proposed work.
- **Reviewer response:** Address the actual concern directly with evidence,
  clarification, or a concrete revision.

For an unspecified request to polish a passage, make local improvements. Do not
silently redefine the contribution or redesign the study. If a substantive issue
cannot be repaired within scope, complete the useful edits and briefly identify
the issue separately.
Preserve the requested format, terminology, citations, and venue constraints.

## Separate Defensive Framing from Necessary Information

Before removing or rewriting a qualification, identify its function:

| Function | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Apology, redundant disclaimer, or imagined objection | Delete if it adds no evidence, scope, or logic. |
| Necessary scope condition | State the applicable population, setting, task, or constraint directly. |
| Uncertainty in the evidence | Retain it with calibrated language; specify its source when known. |
| Methodological limitation or conflicting result | Preserve its substantive meaning and effect on the claim. |
| Conceptual distinction | Keep the contrast when it is needed to understand the argument. |

Place necessary qualifications where readers need them. Consolidate repeated
caveats, but keep a condition beside a claim when separating them would make the
claim misleading, including in the abstract or conclusion.

Do not invent facts, citations, results, mechanisms, representativeness, or
trade-offs to make a rewrite sound stronger. Preserve numerical values,
comparison conditions, and the distinction between association and causation.
An unquantified result does not become significant; an untested explanation
does not become an established mechanism. When support is missing, use a claim
the available material supports or flag the specific information needed.

## Detect Defensive Framing

Actively scan for these common cues, especially at paragraph openings and in
contribution statements. Treat them as prompts to inspect a sentence's function,
not as forbidden strings:

- Disclaimers: "This paper does not claim...", "We do not attempt to...",
  "This is not to say that...", "This should not be taken to mean...".
- Apologetic openings: "Although this study has limitations...",
  "Of course, this does not fully capture...", "While the sample is limited...".
- Protective clarification: "It is worth noting that...", "To be clear...",
  "We acknowledge that...".
- Reflexive contrast: "The goal is not X but Y...", "Rather than arguing X...",
  or repeated "however", "nevertheless", and "although" transitions.
- Stacked hedges and self-judgment: "may possibly suggest", "could potentially",
  "unfortunately", "merely", "only achieves", "limited improvement".

Also look beyond exact wording for repeated scope disclaimers, caveats before
every contribution, and explanations answering objections nobody has raised.
Apply the function-based treatment above: remove empty framing, rewrite useful
content directly, and retain necessary qualifications. In reviewer responses,
an explicit clarification or acknowledgment may directly answer a real concern.

## Revise the Language

Lead with the paragraph's main point. State what the study examines, establishes,
or contributes, then provide the evidence and interpretation needed to support it.

- Remove apology-like judgments such as "unfortunately" or "merely" when the
  factual result communicates the point more accurately.
- Replace stacked hedges with a single calibrated expression. Keep "may,"
  "suggests," or similar language when it reflects genuine uncertainty.
- Prefer positive scope to repeated statements of what the paper does not claim.
  Retain an explicit exclusion when positive scope alone would lose meaning.
- Remove reflexive "not X but Y," "to be clear," and adversative transitions
  when they add no conceptual distinction. Do not impose a blanket word ban.
- Give each paragraph a clear argumentative function. Preserve the author's
  disciplinary voice instead of imposing generic contribution templates.

Examples illustrate decisions, not stock replacement sentences:

- "Unfortunately, our method only achieves 82% accuracy" becomes "Our method
  achieves 82% accuracy." If a relevant baseline achieves 85%, retain that
  comparison; removing the apology does not remove the result.
- "We do not attempt to examine rural settings" can become "The analysis focuses
  on urban settings." If generalizability is at issue, also retain the fact that
  applicability to rural settings has not been evaluated.
- "The evidence may possibly suggest an association" can become "The evidence
  suggests an association." Do not turn this into a causal claim.

## Organize the Narrative and Experiments When in Scope

Identify the most meaningful contribution that the available evidence supports.
It may be a capability, mechanism, finding, method, resource, or useful trade-off;
it need not be superiority on every metric.

Organize the argument around the problem, relevant gap, contribution, evidence,
and significance. Use this as a reasoning structure, not a mandatory section
template. Avoid research-process chronology unless the sequence itself matters
to the method or finding.

State what improves or becomes possible, under which conditions, relative to
which relevant comparison, and why it matters. Support descriptions of prior
work as carefully as descriptions of the contribution.

Give each experiment a clear question: whether the approach works, what explains
the result, where it applies, how it compares under relevant constraints, or
whether an alternative explanation holds. Choose evaluation dimensions for their
relevance to that question. A boundary-finding experiment can be informative
even when it does not favor the method. Distinguish observations, interpretations,
and proposed tests; never imply a proposed experiment has a known outcome.

Remove or relocate redundant and peripheral material while keeping evidence
that materially affects the core claim accessible and reflected in the main text.
Describe an unfavorable result as a trade-off only when both benefit and cost
are supported; otherwise state its implications without broad self-denigration.

When the story exceeds the evidence, narrow or revise the claim and align the
affected sections within the requested scope. Do not redefine success solely
to turn an unfavorable result into a win.

## Apply Section-Specific Judgment

- **Abstract and introduction:** Establish the problem and distinctive contribution
  early. Emphasize the strongest relevant finding and retain essential conditions.
- **Methods and results:** Preserve reproducibility details and report comparisons
  accurately. Remove apologetic commentary without altering the findings.
- **Discussion and limitations:** Explain what the results mean, where the evidence
  stops, and which uncertainties affect use or interpretation. Avoid repeating
  generic admissions that add no information.
- **Conclusion:** Reinforce the supported takeaway and its significance. Avoid
  introducing a new claim or an unrelated catalogue of limitations.
- **Reviewer responses:** Answer each substantive concern before broader framing.
  Acknowledge valid points concisely; support disagreement with evidence. State
  what changed and where when known. Do not invent revisions, analyses, or line
  numbers, and do not evade a requested comparison by changing the subject.

## Final Pass and Delivery

Check that the revision makes the contribution clearer, removes unnecessary
defensiveness, preserves evidence and essential qualifications, and stays within
the requested scope. Check for unsupported strengthening introduced by the edit.

Deliver the revised text in the requested format. Add brief notes only for material
changes, unresolved evidence gaps, or choices the user needs to assess. If the user
asks for text only, return text only. Do not append routine disclaimers, an audit
checklist, or speculative reviewer objections to an otherwise complete revision.
